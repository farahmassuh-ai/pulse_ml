# ML Spec: Pulse Reliability Signal

## 1. Problem statement
Daily commuters use saved trips in Pulse to decide when to leave, but today the app only shows the scheduled time and can't warn them when a bus is likely to be late. Late buses make Pulse feel untrustworthy, and support tickets about "wrong" departure times are our top complaint category. The reliability signal warns riders ahead of time when a saved trip is likely to run late, so they can leave earlier or plan around the delay. It's aimed at the roughly 60% of active users who have saved trips.

## 2. Prediction task
This is a binary classification task: the model predicts whether a bus will be late at a given stop. The unit of prediction is a stop event: one scheduled trip at one timepoint on one service date, which is one row in the historical files. A saved trip maps to the stop event with the same route, direction, stop, and scheduled time that day (saved stops that aren't timepoints are an open question). The target is `late = 1` if the bus's recorded time at the stop is more than 3 minutes after scheduled, else `0`, so late is the positive class. The 3-minute threshold comes from Alex (PM) and simplifies the MBTA's own on-time standard (see open questions). The model outputs P(late), a probability between 0 and 1, and a decision threshold turns it into the reliable/unreliable signal in the app. Task 2 starts with a logistic regression baseline, and I'll try gradient-boosted trees in Task 3.

## 3. Features available at prediction time
A feature has to be known at prediction time, which in this design is the daily refresh before the trip runs. Anything recorded once the trip is underway won't exist yet, so using it would be target leakage. Eleven of the thirteen historical columns are known in advance, and nine of them can be features. `route_id` and `direction_id` identify the route and heading, and `stop_id` and `time_point_id` identify the stop and its timepoint code. `time_point_order` is the timepoint's position in the trip, and `point_type` marks it as the Startpoint, a Midpoint, or the Endpoint. `standard_type` says whether the stop event is judged against the schedule or against headway. `scheduled` is the planned time, and `scheduled_headway` is the planned gap to the previous bus on the same route, in seconds. The Task 2 baselines use seven inputs: route, direction, stop, point type, standard type, and the hour and day of week taken from `scheduled`.

The other two known-in-advance columns aren't features on their own. A raw `service_date` won't generalize to future dates, and `half_trip_id` is an identifier with no predictive signal. In the files, `scheduled` is a Boston local time on a placeholder date (1900-01-01, or 1900-01-02 after midnight), even though it ends in a Z. The loader moves it onto the real service date and adds a day when the placeholder is 1900-01-02. As Task 2 asks, day of week, hour, and minute of day all come from this real `scheduled` time. That changes my first plan, which took day of week from the service date: the 4.6% of rows that run after midnight (341,817 of 7,432,763) now count as the next calendar day at hours 0 to 2, not as the service day they belong to. I can test the service-date version as a feature change in Task 3. The last two columns, `actual` and `headway` (the recorded time and the real gap to the previous bus), only exist after the bus comes through. They can't be inputs, and neither can anything computed from the same stop event's values. `actual` is only used to build the label. Aggregates over earlier stop events, like a stop's past late rate, can be added later if they only use data available at prediction time (the historical files are published monthly).

Every feature needs a source at training time (the historical files) and at serving time (the live API), either directly or through a lookup built from the historical files. At serving time, features come from the MBTA V3 API's `/schedules` endpoint, queried once a day for that day's service, which also meets the PRD's daily-refresh requirement. Its records give route, direction, stop, and trip directly. The scheduled time comes from `departure_time` (or `arrival_time` at a trip's last stop), and hour and day of week come from it the same way as in training. The API doesn't return `time_point_id`, `time_point_order`, `point_type`, or `standard_type`, so they come from a lookup keyed on route, direction, and stop. I checked that key across April to June. Of 2,544 combinations, `time_point_id` never varies, but `time_point_order` varies for 797 (31%), `point_type` for 266 (10%), and `standard_type` for 862 (34%), probably because of branch and short-turn trips and because `standard_type` changes from trip to trip. So the key is only unique for `time_point_id`. For now the daily pull fills `time_point_id`, `time_point_order`, and `point_type` with each combination's most common value and leaves `standard_type` empty. Finding a better key, like trip pattern or time of day, is still open.

The API has no headway field either, so my plan is to compute `scheduled_headway` as the gap to the previous scheduled trip on the same route and direction at that stop, the same way for training and serving to avoid train/serve skew. The historical column wouldn't work anyway: it's empty on every `Headway` row and filled on 94.5% of `Schedule` rows, and each type is about half the data (52% and 48%). The baselines don't use `scheduled_headway` yet.

The two sources also encode values differently, and the daily pull now handles that. It stores `route_id` the way the files do (`01`) and only changes it for the API request, by dropping the leading zero and mapping named routes through the API's route list (for example, SL1 is `741`). It turns direction `0/1` into `Outbound/Inbound`, and it keeps both sources in Boston local time. Eight file routes (191 to 194, CT2, 8026, 8027, and `746_`) have no API route, so they can't be served yet. The API also returns every stop on every trip, and its `timepoint` flag is only set on each trip's first stop, so the pull keeps the route, direction, and stop combinations that appear in the historical files instead of using that flag.

## 4. Label source
Labels come from the historical files, and each row with a non-null `actual` labels itself: `late = 1` if `actual - scheduled` is more than 3 minutes, else `0`, which includes early buses. Both timestamps use the same placeholder-date convention, so the subtraction still works across midnight. Rows with a null `actual` (the bus didn't run, or the logger missed it) are about 6% of the loaded data (451,273 of 7,425,481). They stay in the table but are left out of training and evaluation instead of getting an imputed label. I also dropped rows with a delay outside -30 to +120 minutes, since a delay that extreme looks like a timestamp error. That removed 592 rows across the three months.

## 5. Metrics, baseline, and target
All metrics are reported on held-out data from a time-based split: the model trains on earlier weeks and is evaluated on later weeks, which are divided into validation weeks for tuning and test weeks for the final numbers (Task 2 set them: train is April 1 to May 31, validation is June 1 to 15, and test is June 16 to 30). A random split would put stop events from the same day in both sets and overstate performance. The baseline is a majority-class classifier, which predicts "not late" for everything because late buses are the minority (45.7% of training rows). Its accuracy is 1 minus the late rate of the scored weeks. That's the reference point Alex asked for, and it shows why accuracy is the wrong primary metric: the baseline can look accurate while its recall on the late class is 0. A recall-focused model may even score lower accuracy than the baseline, and that's expected.

Per Alex, the expensive mistake is a false negative: marking a trip reliable when it ends up late. A false positive, flagging a trip that shows up on time, just means the rider leaves a few minutes early. So I'm choosing recall on the late class as the primary metric, since it's the share of late trips we catch. Precision on the late class is the guardrail, because recall alone can be gamed: predicting late for every stop event gives a recall of 1.0 and a useless signal. As Alex put it, "If every trip shows a warning, the warning means nothing."

There's no hard numeric target yet. Alex's 70–80% accuracy figure was explicitly a wish, not a requirement. The data is closer to balanced than I expected, so the majority-class baseline is nowhere near it: on validation it scores 50.8%, and the logistic regression scores 65.7% (AUC 0.72). I'll still report accuracy for context but won't optimize for it. The working target is to beat the baseline where it matters: high recall on the late class at a precision well above the base late rate, since a random classifier's precision equals the base rate (49.2% on validation). I'll propose concrete numbers in Task 3, now that the baselines show where we start.

## 6. Non-goals
Out of scope for this build: predicting the delay in minutes (this is classification, not regression), trip routing, and using live vehicle positions or the MBTA's real-time predictions (the API's `/vehicles` and `/predictions` endpoints). Nothing real-time goes in; the model uses only the schedule and lookups built from the historical files. It also covers buses only, not subway or rail. That's narrower than the PRD's requirement that the signal work for any saved trip in the service area. We'll need a product decision from Alex on what the app shows for saved subway or rail trips (e.g., no signal).

## 7. Open questions
Five things are still open. First, how should rows with no `actual` be handled? Dropping them is my default, but it could bias the data. If they're mostly cancellations, the model never sees the least reliable outcome a rider can get; if they cluster on certain routes, those routes end up underrepresented. For now they're left out of the splits, but I still need to check whether they cluster on certain routes or times before treating that as final.

Second, should headway-standard trips be judged against a timetable at all? About half the rows are `standard_type = Headway`, which the MBTA scores on the gap between buses rather than on schedule adherence, so a timetable-based label may be the wrong target for them. I also checked the MBTA's Service Delivery Policy: even for schedule-standard trips, its on-time window isn't a flat 3 minutes. It allows up to 6 minutes late at mid-route timepoints and 5 at the last stop, and it counts early departures as not on time. So our late rate won't be directly comparable to the MBTA's published numbers, and early buses won't be flagged. Now that we know the late rate (45.7% in training), I'd like to raise both points with Alex.

Third, how does the probability become a yes/no signal? The decision threshold trades precision against recall, so I'll pick it from the precision-recall curve on the validation weeks instead of defaulting to 0.5.

Fourth, should `scheduled_headway` come from the historical column or be recomputed? Task 2 showed the pattern: both columns are blank on about half the rows, and `scheduled_headway` is blank on every `Headway` row. My plan is still to recompute it from scheduled times on both sides.

Fifth, how do we handle saved stops that aren't timepoints? The historical files only cover timepoints, so a trip saved at any other stop has no direct history and would need to be mapped to a nearby timepoint on the same route. That mapping isn't defined yet. The API's `timepoint` flag can't help here, because Task 2 showed it only marks each trip's first stop, so finding which saved stops are timepoints means comparing them against the route, direction, and stop combinations in the historical files.
