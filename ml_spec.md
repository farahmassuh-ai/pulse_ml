# ML Spec: Pulse Reliability Signal

## 1. Problem statement
Daily commuters use saved trips in Pulse to decide when to leave, but today the app only shows the scheduled time and can't warn them when a bus is likely to be late. Late buses make Pulse feel untrustworthy, and support tickets about "wrong" departure times are our top complaint category. The reliability signal warns riders ahead of time when a saved trip is likely to run late, so they can leave earlier or plan around the delay. It's aimed at the roughly 60% of active users who have saved trips.

## 2. Prediction task
This is a binary classification task: the model predicts whether a bus will be late at a given stop. The unit of prediction is a stop event: one scheduled trip at one timepoint on one service date, which is one row in the historical files. A saved trip maps to the stop event with the same route, direction, stop, and scheduled time that day (saved stops that aren't timepoints are an open question). The target is `late = 1` if the bus's recorded time at the stop is more than 3 minutes after scheduled, else `0`, so late is the positive class. The 3-minute threshold comes from Alex (PM) and simplifies the MBTA's own on-time standard (see open questions). The model outputs P(late), a probability between 0 and 1, and a decision threshold turns it into the reliable/unreliable signal in the app. I'll choose the algorithm after EDA in Task 2, likely starting with logistic regression and then trying gradient-boosted trees.

## 3. Features available at prediction time
A feature has to be known at prediction time, which in this design is the daily refresh before the trip runs. Anything recorded once the trip is underway won't exist yet, so using it would be target leakage. Eleven of the thirteen historical columns are known in advance, and nine of them are features. `route_id` and `direction_id` identify the route and heading, and `stop_id` and `time_point_id` identify the stop and its timepoint code. `time_point_order` is the timepoint's position in the trip, and `point_type` marks it as the Startpoint, a Midpoint, or the Endpoint. `standard_type` says whether the stop event is judged against the schedule or against headway. `scheduled` is the planned time, and `scheduled_headway` is the planned gap to the previous bus on the same route, in seconds.

The other two known-in-advance columns aren't raw features. A raw `service_date` won't generalize to future dates, so I'll use it to derive day-of-week instead. Because it's the service day, a 12:30 a.m. trip stays with the day whose schedule it runs on. `half_trip_id` is an identifier with no predictive signal. Hour-of-day comes from `scheduled`, which is stored as a Boston local time on a placeholder date (1900-01-01, or 1900-01-02 after midnight) despite its trailing Z. The remaining two columns, `actual` and `headway` (the recorded time and the actual gap to the previous bus), only exist after the bus comes through. They can't be inputs, and neither can anything computed from the same stop event's values; `actual` is only used to build the label. Aggregates over earlier stop events, like a stop's past late rate, are fine to add later if they only use data available at prediction time (the historical files are published monthly).

Every feature needs a source at both training time (the historical files) and serving time (the live API), either directly or through a lookup built from the historical files. At serving time, features come from the MBTA V3 API's `/schedules` endpoint, queried for each service date every day, which also meets the PRD's daily-refresh constraint. Its records give route, direction, stop, and trip directly. The scheduled time comes as `departure_time` (or `arrival_time` at a trip's last stop), which gives hour-of-day, and day-of-week comes from the service date being queried. The API doesn't return `time_point_id`, `time_point_order`, `point_type`, or `standard_type`, so I'll join them from a lookup keyed on route, direction, and stop. Task 2 has to confirm that key is unique. `standard_type` can vary by trip (the MBTA scores each day's first trip on the schedule standard), and branch or short-turn trips can change a stop's `point_type` or `time_point_order`. If so, the lookup needs extra keys, like trip pattern or time of day. The API has no headway field either, so I'll compute `scheduled_headway` as the gap to the previous scheduled trip on the same route and direction at that stop. Computing it the same way for training avoids train/serve skew and sidesteps the often-blank historical column. The two sources also encode values differently: route `1` vs. `01`, direction `0/1` vs. `Outbound/Inbound`, and ISO timestamps vs. placeholder dates. Task 2 has to normalize these before joining, including putting both sides on Boston local time.

## 4. Label source
Labels come from the historical files, and each row with a non-null `actual` labels itself: `late = 1` if `actual - scheduled` is more than 3 minutes, else `0`, which includes early buses. Both timestamps use the same placeholder-date convention, so the subtraction still works across midnight. Rows with a null `actual` (the bus didn't run, or the logger missed it) make up several percent of a typical month. I'll drop them from training and evaluation rather than impute a label. I'll also drop rows whose delay is outside a sane range, meaning lateness or earliness extreme enough to look like a timestamp error. Task 2 will quantify both groups and set the exact bounds from the delay distribution; this section records intent.

## 5. Metrics, baseline, and target
All metrics are reported on held-out data from a time-based split: the model trains on earlier weeks and is evaluated on later weeks, which are divided into validation weeks for tuning and test weeks for the final numbers (Task 2 sets the exact weeks). A random split would put stop events from the same day in both sets and overstate performance. The baseline is a majority-class classifier, which should predict "not late" for everything since late buses are expected to be the minority. Its accuracy is 1 minus the late rate of the scored weeks. That's the reference point Alex asked for, and it shows why accuracy is the wrong primary metric: the baseline can look accurate while its recall on the late class is 0. A recall-focused model may even score lower accuracy than the baseline, and that's expected.

Per Alex, the expensive mistake is a false negative: marking a trip reliable when it ends up late. A false positive, flagging a trip that shows up on time, just means the rider leaves a few minutes early. So I'm choosing recall on the late class as the primary metric, since it's the share of late trips we catch. Precision on the late class is the guardrail, because recall alone can be gamed: predicting late for every stop event gives a recall of 1.0 and a useless signal. As Alex put it, "If every trip shows a warning, the warning means nothing."

There's no hard numeric target yet. Alex's 70–80% accuracy figure was explicitly a wish, not a requirement. The majority-class baseline might clear it on its own, so I'll report accuracy for context but won't optimize for it. The working target is to beat the baseline where it matters: high recall on the late class at a precision well above the base late rate, since a random classifier's precision equals the base rate. I'll propose concrete numbers once Task 2 shows what the data supports.

## 6. Non-goals
Out of scope for this build: predicting the delay in minutes (this is classification, not regression), trip routing, and using live vehicle positions or the MBTA's real-time predictions (the API's `/vehicles` and `/predictions` endpoints). Nothing real-time goes in; the model uses only the schedule and lookups built from the historical files. It also covers buses only, not subway or rail. That's narrower than the PRD's requirement that the signal work for any saved trip in the service area. We'll need a product decision from Alex on what the app shows for saved subway or rail trips (e.g., no signal).

## 7. Open questions
Five things are still open. First, how should rows with no `actual` be handled? Dropping them is my default, but it could bias the data. If they're mostly cancellations, the model never sees the least reliable outcome a rider can get; if they cluster on certain routes, those routes end up underrepresented. I'll check the pattern in Task 2 before committing.

Second, should headway-standard trips be judged against a timetable at all? About half the rows are `standard_type = Headway`, which the MBTA scores on the gap between buses rather than on schedule adherence, so a timetable-based label may be the wrong target for them. I also checked the MBTA's Service Delivery Policy: even for schedule-standard trips, its on-time window isn't a flat 3 minutes. It allows up to 6 minutes late at mid-route timepoints and 5 at the last stop, and it counts early departures as not on time. So our late rate won't be directly comparable to the MBTA's published numbers, and early buses won't be flagged. I'd like to raise both points with Alex once we know the late rate.

Third, how does the probability become a yes/no signal? The decision threshold trades precision against recall, so I'll pick it from the precision-recall curve on the validation weeks instead of defaulting to 0.5.

Fourth, should `scheduled_headway` come from the historical column or be recomputed? The column and `headway` are blank on a large share of rows, in a pattern that doesn't match the portal's data dictionary. My plan is to recompute it from scheduled times on both sides, but I want to understand the blank pattern in Task 2 first.

Fifth, how do we handle saved stops that aren't timepoints? The historical files only cover timepoints, so a trip saved at any other stop has no direct history and would need to be mapped to a nearby timepoint on the same route. That mapping isn't defined yet, but the API's `timepoint` flag should make it easy to see which saved stops are affected.
